1. Attention-Seeking Behaviors:
Example:
A student frequently calls out answers in class, even when not called upon, and makes inappropriate jokes during instruction.
Possible Function: The behavior may be maintained by the attention received from the teacher and peers, even if it's negative attention.
Intervention: Implement strategies to increase positive attention for appropriate behavior, such as praising the student for raising their hand and participating in class.
Example:
A student consistently disrupts class by talking out of turn or fidgeting excessively.
Possible Function: The behavior may be a way to gain attention from the teacher or peers.
Intervention: Provide the student with opportunities for positive attention by assigning tasks or giving them a role in class that requires their participation. 
2. Escape/Avoidance Behaviors:
Example:
A student refuses to complete assignments or avoids participating in activities that they find difficult or challenging. 
Possible Function: The behavior may be a way to avoid tasks or situations that are perceived as aversive or difficult. 
Intervention: Break down tasks into smaller, more manageable steps, provide extra support or accommodations, or modify the curriculum to meet the student's needs. 
Example:
A student becomes agitated or disruptive when asked to transition from one activity to another. 
Possible Function: The behavior may be an attempt to avoid the transition or a change in routine. 
Intervention: Provide visual cues or timers to signal transitions, allow for brief breaks, or create a predictable daily schedule. 
3. Access to Tangible Items or Activities:
Example:
A student engages in disruptive behaviors to gain access to a preferred activity or item, such as a toy or a specific game.
Possible Function: The behavior may be a way to obtain access to a desired item or activity.
Intervention: Provide opportunities for the student to engage in preferred activities or access desired items in a structured and appropriate manner.
Example:
A student engages in self-stimulatory behaviors, such as rocking or flapping hands, when they are bored or not engaged in an activity.
Possible Function: The behavior may be a way to self-soothe or provide sensory stimulation.
Intervention: Provide opportunities for the student to engage in sensory activities or find alternative ways to meet their sensory needs. 
4. Sensory Functions:
Example:
A student engages in repetitive behaviors, such as rocking or chewing on objects, that provide them with sensory input.
Possible Function: The behavior may be a way to regulate sensory input or provide a calming or stimulating sensation.
Intervention: Provide opportunities for the student to engage in sensory activities or find alternative ways to meet their sensory needs.
Example:
A student becomes agitated or disruptive in response to loud noises or bright lights.
Possible Function: The behavior may be a way to avoid or escape from sensory overload.
Intervention: Modify the environment to reduce sensory stimuli, such as providing a quiet space or dimming the lights.
